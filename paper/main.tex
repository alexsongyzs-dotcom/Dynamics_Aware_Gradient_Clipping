\begin{abstract}
Adaptive gradient clipping can improve training without establishing that its
feedback signals are useful. We examine Dynamics-Aware Gradient Clipping
(DAGC), a bounded threshold controller driven by clipping exposure and
consecutive-gradient alignment. Its exposure term tightens clipping when
clipping is already frequent. We show that an interior target-exposure
equilibrium is unstable in a stationary mean-field model, even with
exponential smoothing. We then compare the frozen controller with tuned
clipping rules, an alignment ablation, and a predetermined threshold schedule
on FashionMNIST and CIFAR-10, using validation-only calibration and twelve
paired confirmation seeds in each of three conditions.

These controls separate threshold scheduling from evidence for an online
feedback mechanism; the analysis does not claim convergence of the coupled
training dynamics.

\section{Introduction}
Gradient clipping limits the contribution of a large gradient to an optimizer
update \citep{pascanu2013difficulty}. A fixed threshold is simple but introduces
a scale-sensitive hyperparameter. Adaptive rules can instead derive a
threshold from gradient history or parameter magnitude
\citep{seetharaman2020autoclip,brock2021high}. When an adaptive method succeeds,
however, its accuracy alone does not establish why: a useful threshold profile,
a smaller effective step size, and informative feedback can produce similar
outcomes.

We examine that ambiguity in Dynamics-Aware Gradient Clipping (DAGC). The
controller uses the recent frequency of clipping and the alignment of
consecutive raw gradients. Its first version, V1, allowed exposure alone to
increase the threshold but not decrease it. V2 replaces that one-sided term
with a term that becomes negative above a reference exposure. This makes
sustained clipping more restrictive. It also gives the feedback the opposite
sign to a clipping-rate tracker: lowering a threshold makes further clipping
more likely.

This distinction changes both the interpretation and the experiment needed to
support the method. A comparison with an arbitrary fixed threshold cannot
establish that exposure or alignment provides useful information beyond the
gradient scale. Removing the alignment term tests its necessity. A lagged
median rule tests whether magnitude information is sufficient. Replaying a
threshold trace constructed on separate calibration runs tests the incremental
value of adapting that threshold to the new training trajectory. Replay still
uses the current norm to apply clipping; only its threshold is predetermined.

Our contribution is an analysis and controlled evaluation of this concrete
controller. First, we derive its local positive-feedback behavior with the
exposure EMA included. Second, we define a reproducible comparison with
validation-only parameter selection, identical paired training streams, and
explicit failure accounting. Third, we preserve the earlier negative mechanism
controls and evaluate transfer to longer training and a residual network on
RGB images. The scope is small image classifiers trained with momentum SGD;
there is no claim of broad optimizer, architecture, or language-model transfer.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Adaptive gradient and update clipping.}
Fixed norm clipping was popularized for recurrent-network optimization
\citep{pascanu2013difficulty}. AutoClip derives a global threshold from
historical norm percentiles \citep{seetharaman2020autoclip}; AGC instead
uses unit-wise parameter magnitude \citep{brock2021high}. ACClip uses
coordinate-wise adaptation in attention models \citep{zhang2020gradient}.
ZClip detects norm spikes through running statistics \citep{kumar2025zclip},
whereas the revised AdaGC bounds tensor gradients relative to historical
clipped-norm estimates \citep{wang2025adagc}. Group-level historical control
also appears in AGGC \citep{li2026aggc}. These rules differ in granularity,
reference scale, and treatment of anomalous gradients. DAGC uses one global
threshold with exposure and consecutive-gradient alignment; its distinction
must be tested against these simpler information sources, not inferred from
the general novelty of an adaptive threshold.

Clippy constrains optimizer updates in multitask ranking
\citep{tang2023clippy}. This distinction matters because raw-gradient clipping
does not directly bound the parameter step after momentum or preconditioning.
StableAdamW addresses underestimated second-moment states in vision-language
training \citep{wortsman2023stableadamw}. Such optimizer-specific protections
motivate our explicit clipping order, but their large-scale results are not
experimental evidence for DAGC.

\paragraph{Rate feedback, quantiles, and robust estimation.}
Adaptive private federated clipping estimates a desired norm quantile online
\citep{andrew2021adaptive}. Quantile clipping has also been studied through
robust stochastic optimization \citep{merad2024quantile} and adaptive
DP-SGD convergence analysis \citep{shulgin2024adaptive}. These analyses
highlight clipping bias and the joint role of threshold and stepsize schedules.
The geometric treatment of private SGD likewise shows that clipping can bias
an update \citep{chen2020geometric}. DAGC is neither a privacy mechanism nor
a quantile estimator: its excess-exposure response has the opposite sign to
rate tracking. Median-gradient estimators connect some clipping methods to
robust location estimation \citep{schaipp2025median}, but estimating a median
gradient vector is distinct from our scalar rolling-median norm control.

\paragraph{Optimization theory and heavy-tailed noise.}
Relaxed-smoothness analyses explain why clipping can accelerate optimization
\citep{zhang2020why,bohang2020improved}. Stability and convergence results
also cover rapidly growing subgradients and clipped momentum
\citep{mai2021stability}. Stochastic bias can limit convergence even when
clipping prevents large updates \citep{koloskova2023revisiting}.
Empirical tail-index studies \citep{simsekli2019tail} and attention-model
analysis \citep{zhang2020gradient} motivate considering non-Gaussian gradient
noise. Accelerated clipping and normalized clipped momentum provide
heavy-tail guarantees under stated assumptions
\citep{gorbunov2020heavy,cutkosky2021tails}; newer work separates the roles
of normalization and clipping \citep{sun2025revisiting}. We neither estimate
a tail index nor verify these assumptions for our networks. These results
supply context, not a convergence theorem for the DAGC controller coupled
to neural-network training.

\paragraph{Adaptation, architecture, and training scale.}
AdaGrad \citep{duchi2011adaptive} and AdaGrad-Norm
\citep{ward2019adagrad} adapt step scales using past gradients; Adam
\citep{kingma2015adam} adds coordinate-wise moment estimates. AdamW
separates weight decay from its adaptive update \citep{loshchilov2019adamw},
while LAMB uses layer-wise update scaling \citep{you2020lamb}. These are
alternative ways to change update magnitude rather than evidence that a
specific clipping feedback signal is informative. Initialization and momentum
also materially affect optimization \citep{glorot2010understanding,sutskever2013importance}.
Batch normalization \citep{ioffe2015batch,santurkar2018batch} and residual
connections \citep{he2016deep} change the training setting, so gains on a
small CNN cannot automatically transfer to a residual model. Small-scale
Transformer proxies reproduce selected instabilities under explicit
conditions \citep{wortsman2024smallscale}; our image experiments do not
establish a corresponding language-model transfer claim.

\paragraph{Evidence for a mechanism.}
Benchmark variance \citep{bouthillier2021accounting}, selection bias
\citep{cawley2010selection}, and finite hyperparameter-search budgets
\citep{bergstra2012random} motivate paired seeds and validation-only selection.
Freshly collected benchmark tests can change absolute scores
\citep{recht2019generalize}; withholding a test set during the new campaign
does not erase earlier exposure to it. Statistical comparisons must respect
the replication unit and test assumptions \citep{dror2018significance}.
We distinguish descriptive legacy evidence, multiplicity-corrected
confirmation \citep{holm1979simple}, and equivalence within a stated
practical margin \citep{lakens2017equivalence}. This prevents a null replay
comparison from being presented either as proof of superiority or as
proof of equivalence.

\section{Controller and Competing Explanations}
Let $g_t=\nabla_\theta\ell_t(\theta_t)$ be the raw minibatch gradient, and let
$n_t=\|g_t\|_2$. A positive threshold $c_t$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde g_t=\alpha_tg_t,\qquad
 \alpha_t=\min\{1,c_t/\max(n_t,\epsilon)\}.
 \label{eq:clip}
\end{equation}
We log exposure $e_t=\mathbf{1}[n_t>c_t]$, intensity $I_t=1-\alpha_t$, and
alignment
$a_t=\langle g_t,g_{t-1}\rangle/\max(n_t n_{t-1},\epsilon)$.
The first alignment is one. The exposure and alignment states are
\begin{align}
 E_t&=\beta E_{t-1}+(1-\beta)e_t,\\
 A_t&=\beta_a A_{t-1}+(1-\beta_a)a_t,
\end{align}
initialized at $E_{-1}=0$ and $A_{-1}=1$. DAGC-V2 updates
\begin{align}
 u_t &= r(\tau-E_t)-w\max(0,-A_t), \label{eq:signal}\\
 c_{t+1}&=\Pi_{[L_t,U_t]}\!\left(c_t\exp[\gamma\tanh(u_t)]\right).
 \label{eq:update}
\end{align}
Here $L_t=\max(0.1m_t,10^{-12})$ and
$U_t=\max(10m_t,10^{-9})$, where $m_t$ is the lower median of the last
200 raw gradient norms, including $n_t$. Thus these bounds affect the next
step. The frozen settings are
$(c_0,\gamma,\beta,\beta_a,r,w,\tau)=(0.5,0.05,0.9,0.9,0.3,3,0.1)$.
V1 replaces $r(\tau-E_t)$ with $r(1-E_t)$; since $E_t\in[0,1]$, its exposure
term cannot itself tighten clipping. V2 can tighten, but this change alone
does not imply useful regulation.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One DAGC-V2 training step}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Compute raw $g_t$, $n_t$, and $a_t$.
\STATE Save raw $g_t$ for the next alignment calculation.
\STATE Apply clipping at the current $c_t$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:clip}.
\STATE Update exposure $E_t$, alignment $A_t$, and norm history.
\STATE Compute $c_{t+1}$ using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:signal}--\eqref{eq:update}.
\STATE Update momentum and parameters using $\widetilde g_t$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The ordering prevents the current norm from selecting its own DAGC threshold.
Clipping precedes SGD's coupled weight decay and momentum accumulation. Hence
$c_t$ bounds the clipped data gradient, not the final parameter displacement.
The method stores a previous full gradient, requiring $O(d)$ additional memory,
and a 200-element norm window. Its diagnostic alignment requires an $O(d)$
dot product. We do not describe it as constant-memory control.

\subsection{The reference exposure is not a stable target}
For a stationary, exogenous gradient-norm distribution, with alignment disabled
and inactive bounds, the exposure-EMA mean-field system has a linearly unstable
interior reference-exposure fixed point whenever the density at that threshold
is positive. Appendix~\ref{app:math} gives the assumptions, Jacobian and complete
proof, together with conditional results on alignment masking and replay.

The result includes exposure smoothing; an EMA does not reverse the feedback
sign. It does not prove instability of neural-network training: the gradient
norm distribution changes with the parameters, the bounds move, and the
alignment signal can activate. It does explain why the reference $\tau$
should not be interpreted as a regulated clipping frequency. Persistent
clipping can instead drive the threshold to its lower bound, where historical
norm scale may dominate the resulting behavior.

\subsection{What clipping can protect}
For momentum SGD without weight decay, $v_t=\mu v_{t-1}+\widetilde g_t$.
If $c_j\leq C$ over $k$ updates and $0\leq\mu<1$, then
\begin{equation}
 \|v_t\|\leq\mu^k\|v_{t-k}\|+
 C\frac{1-\mu^k}{1-\mu}.
\end{equation}
A smaller sustained threshold bounds new momentum contributions but does not
remove an existing buffer. Coupled weight decay adds parameter-dependent
terms. This elementary bound motivates a protection hypothesis without
establishing convergence, explaining a test-accuracy improvement, or requiring
exposure and alignment to be the best threshold signals.

\section{Experimental Design}
\subsection{Questions, conditions, and data separation}
The new study asks whether the frozen controller outperforms tuned fixed
clipping, a tuned median-scale rule, and calibration-trace replay. Its
secondary question is whether removing alignment changes the outcome.
The three conditions are FashionMNIST with the existing small CNN at initial
learning rate $0.8$, and CIFAR-10 with ResNet-20 at $0.1$ or $0.4$.
The latter two are designated lower and higher learning rates; we do not
assume either one diverges without clipping.

FashionMNIST \citep{xiao2017fashion} uses 55,000 training images and a fixed
5,000-image validation split. CIFAR-10 \citep{krizhevsky2009learning} uses
45,000 training and 5,000 validation images. Both splits hold out 500 images
per class using seed 1729. All methods train on the same remaining examples;
validation images never enter training. Only calibration runs evaluate the
validation set. Confirmation runs evaluate the complete official 10,000-image
test set once, after the last training epoch. They retain the same training
partition, preserving the number of steps used to construct replay schedules.
No test score is used to choose a new-campaign hyperparameter or checkpoint.

FashionMNIST uses two convolutional layers (32 and 64 channels), max pooling,
and a 128-unit hidden classifier, with 421,642 parameters. CIFAR-10 uses the
6$n$+2 residual architecture of \citet{he2016deep}, $n=3$, widths 16/32/64,
option-A shortcuts, and batch normalization, with 269,722 parameters. The
RGB model is deeper, not larger in parameter count. This is an architectural
transfer test, not a reproduction of the original ResNet training recipe.

\subsection{Training and calibration budget}
Training uses FP32 SGD with momentum $0.9$, batch size 256, and no dropped
last batch. FashionMNIST runs for 30 epochs with weight decay $10^{-4}$;
CIFAR-10 runs for 60 epochs with weight decay $5\times10^{-4}$. A cosine
schedule decreases the learning rate to 1\% of its initial value. CIFAR-10
uses random padded crops and horizontal flips; FashionMNIST uses normalization
only. All settings are fixed across clipping policies within a condition.

Fixed clipping, AGC, AutoClip, and the median rule each receive three
candidates and three calibration seeds (2100--2102): nine runs per policy
per condition. Their grids are, respectively,
$\{0.05,0.5,5\}$,
$\{0.005,0.03,0.18\}$,
percentiles $\{1,10,50\}$, and
$\{0.025,0.1,0.4\}$.
The winner maximizes mean final validation accuracy, with grid order breaking
exact ties. These equal finite budgets do not constitute exhaustive tuning.
DAGC retains the setting selected during earlier development, so its total
historical tuning budget is not matched to the new baselines.

AGC clips every parameter, grouping matrix and convolution weights by output
unit and treating vectors as a single unit, with parameter-norm floor
$10^{-3}$. AutoClip takes the stated percentile of all raw norms through the
current step, following its authors' convention. The median rule clips at
$\lambda\operatorname{median}(n_{t-200},\ldots,n_{t-1})$, using available
history and initial threshold 0.5. It is causal and uses the same lower-median
convention as DAGC. The no-alignment variant sets $w=0$ without retuning.
Replay averages the thresholds actually applied in three full DAGC calibration
runs and applies this fixed, step-indexed schedule in confirmation. All eight
policies, including no clipping, share a complete training budget.

\subsection{Pairing, failures, and inference}
Confirmation uses twelve seeds, 3100--3111. Within a seed, initial weights,
minibatch order, and augmentations are identical across policies. Private
random generators isolate the input stream from diagnostics and controller
state. Deterministic CUDA operations are enabled and TF32 is disabled. Epoch
checkpoints include model, optimizer, controller, previous raw gradient, and
logs; a test verifies that resumed and uninterrupted runs agree exactly.
Configuration and source checksums reject stale results.

A run stops on nonfinite loss or gradient norm, or loss above $10^6$. A
nonfinite final evaluation also fails. Failed runs receive accuracy zero and
remain in all aggregates; finite chance-level runs retain their observed
score. We do not choose the best epoch, discard failures, or rerun unfavorable
seeds. The final audit checks all 117 calibration and 288 confirmation outcomes.

We report Student-$t$ 95\% intervals over seeds, including paired intervals for
accuracy differences. The primary family comprises DAGC versus fixed, median,
and replay in all three conditions: nine two-sided exact sign-flip tests with
Holm correction. Sign flipping assumes exchangeability under the null; the
$t$ intervals assume the seed-level mean is adequately approximated by that
model. Neither addresses uncertainty from sampling a new dataset. A broad
superiority claim requires a positive effect and corrected significance in
all nine comparisons. Secondary comparisons are descriptive.

A prespecified $\pm0.5$ percentage-point margin supplies an additional
within-comparison equivalence check: the paired 90\% interval must lie entirely
inside that range. This is not a familywise equivalence claim. Nonsignificance
alone is not evidence of equivalence. The original eight-seed plan was amended
to twelve during calibration, before any confirmation runs: with eight seeds,
the minimum two-sided sign-flip $p=2/256$ cannot pass the first nine-test Holm
threshold $0.05/9$. The audit preserves this amendment and all identical
calibration results carried forward from the first execution.

\subsection{Earlier exploratory evidence and corrected interpretation}
The corresponding audited historical plots are retained in
Appendix~\ref{app:legacy-v2-plots}; they are distinct from the confirmation
evidence in Figures~\ref{fig:paired} and~\ref{fig:traces}.

The earlier 15-epoch, 5,000-training/2,000-test experiments used different
training seeds for development and evaluation, but sampled from the same
official test set for both stages. We therefore classify them as exploratory,
not independent data-held-out confirmation. Their positive comparisons to
fixed clipping motivated the present study; they cannot substitute for it.

The most discriminating legacy experiment already limited the mechanism
claim. Over eight paired seeds, DAGC exceeded an intensity-matched fixed rule
by 1.18 points (recomputed 95\% paired interval [0.62, 1.74]), but its advantage
over replay was 0.21 points [$-0.43$, 0.86]. Removing alignment changed accuracy
by 0.16 points [$-0.33$, 0.66], whereas removing exposure cost 5.47 points
[4.04, 6.90]. These results identify the effect of specified ablations on the
whole training trajectory. They do not show that reversal predicts future
loss deterioration or that online feedback adds value beyond replay.

Independent-seed learning-rate perturbations did not resolve the ambiguity:
the DAGC-minus-replay difference was $-0.06$ [$-0.71$, 0.59] for a temporary
learning-rate doubling and $+0.01$ [$-0.51$, 0.52] for a sustained decrease.
The uncertainty spans effects beyond the new equivalence margin, so these
legacy null results are not equivalence claims. Appendix~\ref{app:legacy}
reports all controls and corrected intervals.

A development sweep over the reference exposure had previously pooled two
gains and three seeds into six apparent replicates. We instead report each
gain separately with three seeds. Realized exposure remains at least 0.999
across the grid, while intensity changes. The sweep is consistent with the
positive-feedback interpretation, but its small sample and development role
preclude precise claims of performance insensitivity.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
A controller can influence an optimization trajectory without the proposed
feedback signal being necessary for its gains. The replay comparison removes
threshold adaptation to the confirmation trajectory; the median comparison
retains online magnitude information but removes exposure and alignment;
and the no-alignment comparison removes only direction. Their scopes differ,
so no single one is a complete mechanistic explanation.

\input{generated/discussion_results}

The study remains limited to two image datasets, two small architectures,
one optimizer, and fixed learning-rate schedules. Sixty CIFAR epochs are
longer than the legacy diagnostics but do not establish asymptotic performance
or large-scale training stability. Fixed grids can miss better baseline
settings. Historical FashionMNIST test exposure and unequal earlier method
development remain limitations despite validation-only selection in the new
campaign. Seed intervals are conditional on a fixed data split. Our analytic
model freezes the norm distribution; its unstable controller equilibrium is
not a theorem about the nonlinear coupled training system. Likewise, lower-bound
occupancy and accuracy correlation cannot identify a causal mechanism alone.

Appendix~\ref{app:math} proves conditional results on projection masking,
exact same-run replay, and the value of causal information. These results do
not establish that DAGC realizes an optimal feedback policy or outperforms
independent-calibration replay; the confirmation results do not support either
claim.

\section{Conclusion}
DAGC-V2 implements positive exposure feedback, not stable target-rate tracking
in the stationary mean-field model. A useful empirical claim must therefore
survive comparisons with magnitude-only adaptation and a predetermined
threshold schedule, in addition to tuned fixed clipping. Our protocol makes
those comparisons explicit, preserves failure outcomes, and separates legacy
exploration from current validation-only selection.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work examines optimization behavior in small image classifiers. It does
not deploy a model or introduce a new dataset. Better-controlled evaluation
can reduce wasted experiments and unsupported claims about training stability.
A clipping rule is not a guarantee of reliable or safe behavior in downstream
applications; broader deployment consequences depend on the model and use.

\section{Failures and Clipping Diagnostics}
For global clipping, the logged coefficient is the scalar multiplier. AGC
instead logs the ratio of the whole clipped-gradient norm to the raw norm;
its value does not imply a global scalar intervention, since individual units
receive different multipliers. Frequency counts ratios below $1-10^{-7}$.
Intensity is one minus that ratio. Bound occupancy counts whether the newly
computed DAGC threshold equals its lower bound to relative tolerance $10^{-6}$.
For other policies this diagnostic is zero by construction and does not imply
that their thresholds are unconstrained.

\section{Mathematical Mechanism: Exact Results and Their Limits}
\label{app:math}
This appendix separates implementation-level facts from conditional statements
about the value of feedback. None establishes unconditional test-accuracy
superiority of DAGC or its alignment term. In particular, a theorem about an
optimal feedback policy is not a theorem that DAGC realizes that policy.

\subsection{Exposure feedback with exponential smoothing}
Use the stationary, exogenous norm distribution of Section 3, with inactive
bounds and no alignment term. Define $x_t=\log c_t$ and
$q(x)=1-F(e^x)$. The deterministic mean-field recursion is
\begin{align}
 E_t&=\beta E_{t-1}+(1-\beta)q(x_t),\\
 x_{t+1}&=x_t+\gamma\tanh[r(\tau-E_t)].
\end{align}
\begin{proposition}
Suppose $0\leq\beta<1$, $\gamma r>0$, and an interior fixed point
$c_*>0$ satisfies $1-F(c_*)=\tau$ and $f(c_*)>0$. The mean-field fixed point
is linearly unstable.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $s=c_*f(c_*)>0$ and $k=\gamma r(1-\beta)s>0$.
For the state $(x_t,E_{t-1})$, the Jacobian at the fixed point is
\begin{equation}
 J=\begin{pmatrix}
 1+k&-\gamma r\beta\\
 -(1-\beta)s&\beta
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Its characteristic polynomial is
$p(\lambda)=\lambda^2-(1+\beta+k)\lambda+\beta$.
Since $p(1)=-k<0$ and $p(\lambda)\to+\infty$ as
$\lambda\to+\infty$, it has a real root greater than one.
\end{proof}
The positive eigenvalue exceeding one proves local instability of this
mean-field equilibrium only. Moving bounds, alignment, and the evolving
training distribution are excluded assumptions, not small perturbations
whose effects have been proved negligible.

\subsection{What the direction penalty provably changes}
\begin{proposition}[One-step monotonicity and saturation]
Compare two controller updates from the same state $c,E,A$, with the same
positive bounds $L\leq U$, gains $\gamma\geq0$, $w\geq0$, and reference
signal $b=r(\tau-E)$. Let
\begin{align}
 c^{(0)}&=\Pi_{[L,U]}\big(c\exp[\gamma\tanh b]\big),\\
 c^{(A)}&=\Pi_{[L,U]}\big(c\exp[\gamma\tanh(b-wz)]\big),\quad
 z=\max(0,-A).
\end{align}
Then
\begin{equation}
 0\leq\log c^{(0)}-\log c^{(A)}\leq\gamma wz.
\end{equation}
They are equal when $z=0$, or when both proposals saturate at the same bound.
If $\gamma wz>0$ and both proposals lie strictly inside the bounds,
$c^{(A)}<c^{(0)}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In log coordinates, projection is the monotone, 1-Lipschitz map
$P(x)=\min(\log U,\max(\log L,x))$. The unprojected difference is
$\gamma[\tanh b-\tanh(b-wz)]$. Monotonicity and the derivative bound
$0<\tanh'(x)\leq1$ place it in $[0,\gamma wz]$; applying $P$ preserves
order and cannot increase the difference. Strictness holds without projection
saturation because $\tanh$ is strictly increasing.
\end{proof}
This result concerns the next threshold at a shared state. It does not order
the full trajectories after the two policies change their gradients, exposures,
and bounds. A negative instantaneous alignment is also not sufficient for
activation: the implemented penalty uses the smoothed state $A$.

\begin{proposition}[Inactive direction term gives identical training]
Start full DAGC and its no-alignment variant with identical model, optimizer,
controller, and random-stream states. If the full run has $A_t\geq0$ at every
controller update, their thresholds, model states, and losses are identical
at every step.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At the initial shared state the direction penalty is zero. Both policies use
the same current threshold and sample, hence the same raw and clipped
gradients. Their exposure, norm history, moving bounds, and optimizer updates
are identical, giving the same next state. Induction repeats this argument;
the hypothesis makes the full policy's direction penalty zero at each step.
\end{proof}
Thus no strictly positive directional benefit can hold uniformly over all
training problems. Merely observing a nonzero penalty establishes intervention,
not improved loss or generalization.

\subsection{Exact replay equivalence and its scope}
\begin{proposition}[Replay of a run's own applied thresholds]
Consider any deterministic execution conditional on its initial state and
complete random stream, including minibatches and augmentation. Save the
thresholds actually applied by a feedback run. A replay run using that exact
step-indexed trace, the same initial model/optimizer state, and the same random
stream has identical raw gradients, clipped gradients, optimizer states,
parameters, and evaluations at all steps.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At step zero both executions have the same parameters and training sample,
so their raw gradients agree. The replayed applied threshold makes clipping
identical. The deterministic optimizer update, including coupled weight decay
and momentum, then produces identical next states. Repeat by induction. The
replay need not reconstruct controller states; it only reproduces its applied
thresholds, and its diagnostics must not alter the training random stream.
\end{proof}
This oracle trace comes from the very run being reproduced and is not our
independent-calibration comparator. The proposition therefore neither proves
nor disproves a benefit over calibration-trace replay. It does prove that a
successful feedback trajectory can be reproduced by a schedule when the
schedule contains the same realized decisions. In a problem where feedback
produces the same trace for every seed, independent replay is also identical;
universal strict dominance of feedback over replay is consequently impossible.
Replay still responds to the current gradient norm through its clipping
coefficient; it is not a fixed learning-rate multiplier.

\subsection{When pre-gradient information can strictly help}
We now give a sufficient-condition analysis that respects causal threshold
selection. It compares policies at a common random checkpoint, not policies
whose earlier training has already produced different states.
Let the objective be $f(\theta)=\kappa\|\theta\|^2/2$, $\kappa>0$.
Let $v$ denote the previous momentum buffer, $\mu\in[0,1)$, and
$\eta>0$ the shared step size. Set weight decay to zero in this model.
A policy chooses a threshold $c$ from $[l,u]$, $0<l<u$, using information
$\mathcal F$ available before the next stochastic gradient $G$ is drawn.
Assume $\|G\|>u$ almost surely, so every admissible policy actively clips.
Write $U=G/\|G\|$, and suppose all second moments needed below are finite.
Then the actual momentum update is
\begin{equation}
 \theta^+=\theta-\eta(\mu v+cU),\qquad \|U\|=1.
\end{equation}
Define the scalar conditional prediction
\begin{equation}
 H_{\mathcal F}=
 \mathbb E[\langle\theta/\eta-\mu v,U\rangle\mid\mathcal F].
 \label{eq:conditional-H}
\end{equation}
The random state and the future gradient have the same joint distribution
for every policy in this one-step comparison. Policies may change $c$, but
may not change that checkpoint distribution or use the future $G$ to choose it.

\begin{proposition}[Optimal causal threshold and approximation cost]
In this model the optimal $\mathcal F$-measurable threshold is
$c^*_{\mathcal F}=\Pi_{[l,u]}H_{\mathcal F}$. If
$l<H_{\mathcal F}<u$ almost surely, any admissible feedback rule $c_D$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E f(\theta_D^+)-\mathbb E f(\theta_*^+)
 =\frac{\kappa\eta^2}{2}
   \mathbb E(c_D-H_{\mathcal F})^2.
 \label{eq:approx-cost}
\end{equation}
Without the interior assumption, the difference is at least
$\kappa\eta^2\mathbb E(c_D-c^*_{\mathcal F})^2/2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expanding the squared norm gives
\begin{equation}
 f(\theta^+)=\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\theta-\eta\mu v\|^2
 +\frac{\kappa\eta^2}{2}c^2
 -\kappa\eta^2c\langle\theta/\eta-\mu v,U\rangle.
\end{equation}
Take conditional expectations and use measurability of $c$. The terms
depending on the chosen threshold become
$\kappa\eta^2[(c-H_{\mathcal F})^2-H_{\mathcal F}^2]/2$.
Pointwise minimization over $[l,u]$ yields projection. In the interior the
optimal squared term vanishes, proving Eq.~\eqref{eq:approx-cost}. For a
projected optimum, the projection inequality
$(c_D-c^*)(c^*-H_{\mathcal F})\geq0$ yields the stated lower bound after
expanding the difference of squares.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Exact benefit over a predetermined threshold]
Under the interior assumptions above, the best constant threshold at that
step is $\bar c=\mathbb E H_{\mathcal F}$. Its advantage relative to any
admissible causal rule $c_D$ is exactly
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E f(\theta_{\bar c}^+)-\mathbb E f(\theta_D^+)
 =\frac{\kappa\eta^2}{2}
 \left[\operatorname{Var}(H_{\mathcal F})
       -\mathbb E(c_D-H_{\mathcal F})^2\right].
 \label{eq:feedback-gain}
\end{equation}
Hence the feedback rule strictly improves expected one-step loss if and only
if its squared approximation error is smaller than
$\operatorname{Var}(H_{\mathcal F})$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A constant policy minimizes
$\mathbb E(c-H_{\mathcal F})^2$ at $c=\mathbb E H_{\mathcal F}$, which
is admissible because $H_{\mathcal F}\in(l,u)$ almost surely.
Its minimum value is $\operatorname{Var}(H_{\mathcal F})$. Subtract the
two risks using Eq.~\eqref{eq:approx-cost}.
\end{proof}
A calibration-generated replay value is constant across new runs at a fixed
step, conditional on the independent calibration archive. The result applies
to such a common-state, one-step comparison if its other assumptions hold.
It does not apply directly to complete DAGC/replay runs with different states,
moving bounds, or intermittently inactive clipping.

\subsection{Exact value of additional directional information}
\begin{proposition}[Information gain versus policy error]
Let $\mathcal F_0\subseteq\mathcal F_1$ be two pre-gradient information sets;
the latter may add stored alignment history. Define $H_j$ by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:conditional-H} using $\mathcal F_j$, and assume
$l<H_j<u$ almost surely for $j=0,1$. The difference between the optimal
policies is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E f(\theta_{H_0}^+)-\mathbb E f(\theta_{H_1}^+)
 =\frac{\kappa\eta^2}{2}\mathbb E(H_1-H_0)^2.
 \label{eq:direction-information}
\end{equation}
For actual policies $c_0,c_1$ measurable with respect to their respective
information sets, the exact difference is
\begin{align}
 &\mathbb E f(\theta_{c_0}^+)-\mathbb E f(\theta_{c_1}^+)\\
 &\quad=\frac{\kappa\eta^2}{2}\left[
 \mathbb E(H_1-H_0)^2+\mathbb E(c_0-H_0)^2
 -\mathbb E(c_1-H_1)^2\right].
\end{align}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The tower property gives
$\mathbb E(H_1\mid\mathcal F_0)=H_0$.
Since $H_0$ and $c_0$ are $\mathcal F_0$-measurable,
$\mathbb E[(c_0-H_0)(H_1-H_0)]=0$. Thus
$\mathbb E(c_0-H_1)^2=
\mathbb E(c_0-H_0)^2+\mathbb E(H_1-H_0)^2$.
Use the expanded risk conditional on $\mathcal F_1$ and subtract the two
policies. Taking $c_j=H_j$ proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:direction-information}.
\end{proof}
Additional alignment information helps the ideal policy strictly only if it
changes the conditional prediction on a set of positive probability.
Even then a particular direction penalty can lose that benefit through policy
approximation error. Its sign and coefficient cannot be justified by the
mere availability of directional information.

\subsection{Why threshold tightening alone is not a proof of improvement}
Set $f(\theta)=\theta^2/2$, $\theta=1$, $v=0$, $U=G=1$.
Both $c_h=0.8$ and $c_l=0.4$ actively clip. At $\eta=0.1$, their next
losses are 0.4232 and 0.4608: tightening increases loss by 0.0376.
At $\eta=3$, losses are 0.98 and 0.02: tightening decreases loss by 0.96.
A negative stored alignment does not identify which step-size regime holds.
These one-step examples refute universal benefit from tightening; they are
not complete DAGC training trajectories.

\paragraph{What remains unproved for DAGC.}
DAGC has not been shown to predict $H_{\mathcal F}$ accurately enough,
to obtain incremental information from alignment, or to satisfy this model's
assumptions throughout training. G1 estimates a different risk target.
Consequently, these conditional proofs explain when feedback can help;
they do not establish DAGC's empirical incremental benefit or generalization.
Paired replay, median, no-alignment, and bound-only controls remain needed.
